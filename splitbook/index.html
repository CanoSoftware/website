What it keeps
01

A plan toward a race

Set the race and your A, B and C goals, then build the block week by week. Copy a week forward with a percentage added, write structured workouts with paces and repeats, or import a plan from a spreadsheet. Between races, a base-building block keeps the weeks going.

02

Runs, sorted for you

Import FIT, GPX or TCX files from your watch, or read runs from Apple Health. If the same run arrives twice, the more accurate copy wins and nothing you typed is lost. Each run gets its likely type (easy, tempo, intervals, long) and you confirm it with one tap.

03

The numbers a coach would ask for

Splits, grade-adjusted pace, heart rate zones and relative effort on every run. Across the block: training load, race time predictions, best efforts and how your PRs have moved, heartbeats per mile on easy days, and heart rate drift on long runs.

04

Computed, not guessed

Every metric comes from a written-down formula with tests behind it, worked out on your iPhone. On iPhones with Apple Intelligence, it can write a short summary of a run, using only numbers the app already calculated. Without it, nothing is missing.

05

Race day, and the year

A readiness check on the next race, the weather you ran in and the forecast for race morning, shoe mileage, home screen widgets for the countdown and the week, and a year in review when December comes around.

Privacy

Private by default.

Splitbook uses a server, which most of my apps don't. Apple doesn't allow health data in iCloud, so runs sync through a database I run. Before anything leaves your phone, routes, GPS tracks and the original files are encrypted with a key only your devices hold. I can't see where you ran.

Summary numbers like distance, pace and heart rate are stored unencrypted so the app can total and sort them. They're locked to your account, but as the person running the server I could technically read them. I don't look. There are no ads, no analytics and no tracking in the app.
